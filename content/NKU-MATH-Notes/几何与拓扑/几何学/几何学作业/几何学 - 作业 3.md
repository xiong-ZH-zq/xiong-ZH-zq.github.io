>[!faq] T1
> Choose any $\vec{v}\in \overrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{2}$ , 
> (a) Show that given any $f\in \operatorname{Isom}\mathbb{R}^{2}$ , the composition $f\circ T_{\vec{v}}\circ f^{-1}$ is a translation. Find the translation vector associated to this translation.
> 
> (b) Draw one example and use it to explain the statement in the first question.

***Proof***

(a) Without loss of generality, let $f$ be an isometry that fix $O\in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ . (If not we can let $f = T_{\vec{v}}\circ f'$ and $f'$ will be the isometry that fix $O$). Then $f$ will be a linear map.

Thus,
$$
f[(T_{\vec{v}}\circ f^{-1}) (x)] = f[f^{-1} (x) + \vec{v}] = x+\vec{v}
$$
Then the composition is a translation and translation vector will be $\vec{v}$ .

(b) See the example below. Consider the $30^{\circ}$ rotation by clockwise, trainslation $\vec{v}=(0,1)^{\mathrm{T}}$ .

>[!faq] T2
> Show that all isometries of $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ can be written as compositions of reflections.

***Proof***

It is sufficient to prove the cases when isometries are translations and rotations.

When $f\in \operatorname{Isom}\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is a **translation**, consider $f(x) = x+v$ . Let $A$ be the reflection matrix which satisfies $Av = -v$ . Then
$$
f_{1}(x) = Ax+v, f_{2}(x) = Ax
$$
are all reflections. Then
$$
f_{1}\circ f_{2}(x) = A(Ax) + v = x+v = f
$$
When $f\in \operatorname{Isom}\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is a **rotation**, say
$$
r(x) = B(x-p) + p 
$$
where $B$ is a rotation matrix and $p\in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ . Then we have
$$
r(x) = t\circ h\circ t^{-1}
$$
where $t(x) = x+p$ is a translation and $h$ is a rotation around $O$ . Then we only need to consider $h$ can be written as compositions of reflections. Let $B'$ be any reflection matrix and we have
$$
g_{1}(x) = BB'x, g_{2}(x) = B'x
$$
Both of them are reflections. And $g_{1}\circ g_{2}(x) = Bx$ , this means $h$ can be written as composition of two reflections. $\square$

>[!faq] T3
>Let $\underline{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ and $r\in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ . Let $\underline{y}\in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ be such that $\underline{x}\neq \underline{y}$ .
> (a) Show that the distance between $\underline{y}$ and $C_{\underline{x},r}$ the circle centered at $\underline{x}$ of radius $r$ can be realized by a unique point $\underline{z}$ in $C_{\underline{x},r}$ i.e. there is a unique point $\underline{z}\in C_{\underline{x},r}$ such that
> $$
> d_{\mathbb{E}}(\underline{y},\underline{z}) = d_{\mathbb{E}}(\underline{y},C_{\underline{x},r})  
> $$
> (b) Show that the points $\underline{x},\underline{y},\underline{z}$ are collinear, i.e. on a same line in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ .

***Proof***

(a) Without generality, let $\underline{x}=0$ (By translation).

Consider
$$
f(z) = d_\mathbb{E}(\underline{y},z), z\in C_{\underline{x},r}
$$
This is a continuous function and $C_{\underline{x},r}$ is a closed set. Then $f(z)$ is bounded and has its minimum value on $C_{\underline{x},r}$ . We can denote that
$$
f(\underline{z}) = \min_{z\in C_{\underline{x},r}}f(z)
$$
About uniqueness we can consider
$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{2}(z) & = d^{2}_{\mathbb{E}}(\underline{y},z) = (y_{1}-z_{1})^{2}+(y_{2}-z_{2})^{2} \\
& =  (y_{1}-r\cos \theta)^{2}+(y_{2}-r \sin \theta)^{2} \\
& = r^{2} +y_{1}^{2}+ y_{2}^{2} - 2ry_{1}\cos \theta - 2ry_{2}\sin \theta \\
& = C- 2r\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}}}\sin (\theta + \gamma), \theta\in [0, 2\pi]
\end{aligned}
$$
where $z = (z_{1},z_{2}) = (r\cos \theta,r\sin \theta)$ . We can see $f(z)$ reaches its minimum if and only if $\theta+\gamma = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ , which make $\theta$ unique.

(b) By triangle inequality we can get
$$
d_\mathbb{E}(\underline{y},\underline{z}) \leqslant d_\mathbb{E} (\underline{y},\underline{z}) + d_{\mathbb{E}}(\underline{z},\underline{x})
$$
when equality holds we can get they are collinear. $\square$


>[!faq] T4
> Let $f,g\in \operatorname{Isom} \mathbb{R}^{2}$ . Let $\underline{x},\underline{y}$ and $\underline{z}$ be three pairwise distinct points in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ which are not collinear. Show the equivalence between the following two statements:
> 
> (a) We have $f=g$ .
> 
> (b) We have $f(\underline{x}) = g(\underline{x}), f(\underline{y})=g(\underline{y})$ and $f(\underline{z})=g(\underline{z})$ at the same time.

***Proof***

It is sufficient to prove (b) $\Rightarrow$ (a) .

We have $f(\underline{x}) = g(\underline{x}), f(\underline{y})=g(\underline{y})$ then we can say $f$ and $g$ has the same effect to the basis in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ . If $f$ and $g$ fix the origin $O$ we say they are identical.

If they don't fix the origin $O$ then we can apply same translation $T_{\vec{\underline{z}}}$ to make them fix $O$ since they have $f(\underline{z})=g(\underline{z})$ . Then we reduce the problem to the case above. $\square$
